How we work

How an engagement runs, from first conversation to signed agreement.

This is how a full deal mandate runs. A second opinion can be an hour on the phone; reports and part-time roles take the parts they need.

01

First conversation

It starts with where the project actually is, not where the pitch deck says it is.

We talk through the technology, the site, the feedstock or power supply, the product, who you have already spoken to and what your lenders or investors are asking for. By the end, both sides should know whether there is a commercial problem we can help solve, and what solving it would involve.

Most projects that stall do so for the same reason: the plant can be built, but nobody has yet signed to buy what it makes, on terms a lender will accept. That is usually the conversation.

02

Scoping the engagement

We agree what the work needs to deliver, and by when, before any of it starts. Typically that means:

  • The buyer map: who could take the fuel, what obligation drives each of them (ReFuelEU, FuelEU Maritime, the UK SAF Mandate, RED III, the RFS, the LCFS) and how much they might commit to.
  • What financing needs: how much of the output must be contracted, for how long and with what credit behind it.
  • The pricing basis: what the plant needs to earn, and which pricing structures could get the buyer there.
  • The plan and timetable: who to approach first, in what order, timed around the financing.
03

Structuring and negotiation

This is where most of the work happens. We turn the plan into term sheets and take them to buyers: pricing formulas, volumes and flexibility, take-or-pay, credit support, how certificates and credits are shared, and what happens if the rules change.

The aim is a structure the buyer can sign, the lender can finance and the producer can live with for five years or more. Getting all three to agree usually means trading flexibility for certainty, one term at a time.

04

Signing and beyond

A signed term sheet is not the end. The long-form contract has to carry the deal through lender due diligence and financial close, and the feedstock and power contracts on the other side have to match it on tenor, pricing and risk.

After signing, contracts need looking after: price reviews, annual nominations, and renegotiation when regulation or markets move. We stay involved where that is useful.

What makes a deal work

Four recent low-carbon fuel deals, and what decided them. The pattern is the same each time: projects that close have a buyer committed to a volume, on terms a lender can finance.

Worked · e-methanol, Denmark

Kassø: small, early, with committed buyers

European Energy’s Kassø plant began producing e-methanol in May 2025, with capacity of up to 42,000 tonnes a year. Its first buyers were A.P. Moller-Maersk, the LEGO Group and Novo Nordisk.

Why: Buyers with their own climate targets were willing to commit early, and the plant was sized to the demand that actually existed.

Worked · green methanol, China

Maersk and Goldwind: one anchor buyer, at scale

In November 2023, Maersk agreed to buy 500,000 tonnes a year of green methanol, bio and e-methanol, from a new wind-powered Goldwind facility in north-east China, with production expected from 2026.

Why: A single buyer with ships on order took a large, long volume, which gave the producer the certainty to invest. Allowing both bio and e-methanol gave room to deliver.

Worked · green ammonia, Germany

H2Global: closing the price gap in public

In July 2024, Fertiglobe won Germany’s first H2Global tender to supply renewable ammonia from Egypt at €1,000 a tonne, with a minimum of 40,000 tonnes a year from 2028 to 2033. An intermediary buys long-term from the producer and resells to buyers, with public funding covering the difference.

Why: Separating the long-term purchase from the shorter-term sale let a producer get a bankable contract while buyers were not yet ready to commit for ten years.

Stalled · e-methanol, Sweden

FlagshipONE: built to be big, but no buyers

Ørsted took a final investment decision on FlagshipONE in December 2022 and broke ground in 2023, then stopped the project in August 2024, saying the e-fuel market was developing more slowly than expected. It had not secured long-term offtake.

Why: Funding, subsidies and political support were not enough without signed buyers, and EU rules did not yet oblige shipping lines to buy e-fuels.
