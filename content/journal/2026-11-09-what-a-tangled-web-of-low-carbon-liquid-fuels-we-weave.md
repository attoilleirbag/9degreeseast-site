600 million years ago, tourism on Earth would have had quite a few 1-star reviews. It was a planet filled with a dull monoculture of mostly single-celled microbes floating through the sea. 80 million years later, the sea had become a riot of multi-cellular, now large enough to see, life forms spanning across a bizarre mix of over 30 body structures. *Opabinia* was a standout with 5 eyes on stalks gathered on its head, and an extended proboscis one-third the length of its body ending in a claw. Environmental triggers had somehow supercharged evolution into this circus of experimentation known as the Cambrian Explosion. And then by 500 million years ago, the vast majority of animal life had settled into the limited set of basic body structures we know even today; mainly arthropod (insects), chordate (humans), and molluscs (snails, octopuses). 

Back on present day Earth, low carbon liquid fuels are undergoing their own Cambrian event. Technologies are being designed, invented, and redeployed in a dozen new approaches to breaking down our imperfect feedstock substitutes and building them back together as dense energy-rich oil product replacements. We are focussing on agricultural wastes, the tough ligno-celluloses nature builds to shoot trees into the skies, wood wastes, and even a manure or two. These are then taking a homogenous base Intermediate; a flow of synthetic gas, the universe’s lightest element hydrogen, and even the archenemy Carbon Dioxide; and chemically reconstructing them into perfect, dense, low carbon drop-in fuels that function in the world’s existing infrastructure. It’s a spaghetti bowl of technologies, each with a complicated cost structure bound up in volatile feedstocks, major capital expenditure (capex) programmes, and a maze through commercialisation.  Every project developer hopes for a eureka-moment manufacturing system that hits at the lowest cost replacement of oil; the equivalent of evolution’s final organised focus on arthropods, chordates and molluscs. But given the uncertainties in feedstock availability, capex costs, operational efficiencies and a hundred other variables that over time will likely determine a few winners, I wouldn’t rule out a five-eyed *Opabinia* or two placing in the finals. There is nothing obvious in the energy transition.

*The Oil Monoculture*

Oil isn’t just dense and efficient. Much like ancient Planet Microbe, it’s wonderfully monocultural. Oil has different specifications but is mainly a single feedstock recovered from reserves and refined into a fairly limited slate of useful products. Natural gas is even more easily processed and delivered to pipelines ready for consumption. The size of the industry is so vast, that oil, diesel, gasoline, really the entire slate of products, are each large enough in their own right to scale, ship and trade. This size logistically and economically matters; it makes an already relatively inexpensive feedstock connect the best supply to the best local refining and then onwards, to match refined products to local demand in an astonishing ballet of efficiency. And the homogenous product slate, in stasis for decades, means the whole of human technology can be designed to combust these always available fuels. A car runs on gasoline and a container ship runs on VLSFO, both in stock from your neighbourhood petrol station or your friendly port sales depot.  

Replacing this system with low carbon alternatives takes the tidy oil and gas monoculture and replaces it with a riot of new feedstocks and new technologies; a batch of innovative new Intermediates all to land back into the then familiar product slate of gasoline, diesel and so on. Our single process from a barrel of oil into the entire slate of product we need is replaced by only pieces of the barrel, often a single product at a time. The low carbon goal is to get back to this slate of either chemically identical fuels or versions that can at least blend in and substitute for their energy. But what was a clean feedstock story has to become less efficient. Volumes are broken into smaller lots, low carbon fuel plants are distributed where their smaller feedstocks are available, new smaller logistics chains need establishing. Green fuels professionals may love the intricacies and complexities of this new world but there’s no denying it’s messier.        

*We Can Do This the Easy Way or We Can Do This the Hard Way*

How do we think about the low carbon fuels world clearly? The big view is that the low carbon fuel industry starts with Feedstock, takes Technology to transform these into Intermediates, then takes additional Technology to finalise the Intermediates into Product. Occasionally Intermediates can be useful Products, when consumed in their final form as substitutes for Fossil products. The Feedstock, the Intermediates and the Products all have Fossil equivalents and by the time we have manufactured Products these should more or less cleanly substitute for or blend into Fossil now in use. 

Committing further to this criminal simplification, we have two basic categories of Feedstocks and Technology; Easy and Hard. Easy Feedstocks are in a form that is easily transformed by Easy Technology to Intermediates or Products. The Easy Feedstocks to Product pipeline means the Feedstocks are in a chemical form that converts without huge energy cost or complication in an Easy Technology that is proven to work. Hard Technology is either novel and unproven at scale, or delicate requiring more operational runtimes to prove or refine, or requires material capex backed by major corporate funding. The Hardest Technologies are often mired in all three challenges simultaneously.    

The energy system now produces 120 million oil-equivalent tonnes of low carbon fuels. The IEA’s Net Zero scenario requires an additional 730 million tonnes. I regret to inform you that 105 million tonnes of these 120 million tonnes are Easy Feedstocks being transformed by Easy Technology into useful blending Intermediates or Products. This is the world of ethanol and biodiesel and has been the modern world’s largest low carbon fuels market, built over the last several decades. Ethanol is produced by the fermentation of starches and sugars (mainly corn, sugarcane and wheat) and humans have been doing this since our ancestors cracked open their first beer at the end of a hard day busy Transitioning from hunter-gathering to farming. Biodiesel is produced from vegetable oils having their glycerol swapped for methanol (green or fossil) in a transesterification process that can be pulled off in your garage (not an endorsement). Most low carbon liquid fuels on Earth are thus, Easy Feedstocks and Easy Technology: Easy-Easies. Generally ethanol and biodiesel are less compatible fuels that are blended into fossil gasoline and diesel and limited to certain percentages due to their technical specifications so they’re better described as useful Intermediates, though there are exceptions depending on the engine they’re routed towards.   

There is an additional 15 million tonnes of Products (a slate of products including naphtha, diesel, and jet fuel) being produced from Easy Feedstocks (vegetable oils) using Hard Technology. This is the Hydroprocessed Esters and Fatty Acids (HEFA) industry, which is an upgrade to transesterification in that it uses hydrogen, strips out water and CO2 and ultimately produces a long, straight chain hydrocarbon that is almost indistinguishable from the fossil equivalent. This is a proper Product made from a Hard Technology (industrial-scale capex commitments, complex, modestly energy intensive, impossible in your garage). In terms of vocabulary, these products will be referred to as HEFA products, the diesel is often called HVO in Europe (Hydrotreated Vegetable Oil) and Renewable Diesel in the US. Miles or kilometres, this is the same Technology and Product. So now we have our Easy-Easies and some additional Easy-Hards.     

The Easy-Easies and increasingly the Easy-Hards are the quiet backbone of the low carbon fuel world. They’ve reached material volumes, they’re produced globally, they ship and trade and show up in a liquid price index you can buy and sell on. So if this has clearly worked and feels quite simple, why on Earth is most of the low carbon fuel universe buzzing with work on electricity, carbon capture, synthetic gas, electrolysis, and a dozen other concepts not usually associated with fuel? Because regulators hate Easy Feedstocks often for good reason, their carbon reduction is often mediocre, and there aren’t enough of them anyway. The Easy Feedstocks (starches, sugars and oils) are also food and Earth is a fairly optimised agricultural system where additional growing will likely compete for these food resources by increasing prices. There are simply limits to the Easy Feedstock growth and if we need 850 million tonnes of total low carbon fuel, at 105 million tonnes we’re probably sitting at near the maximum the Easy-Easies can provide. There is some room left for HEFA. Regulations that discourage Easy Feedstocks do make an exception for waste versions of these Feedstocks (such as Used Cooking Oil) as well as beneficial cover crops whose use does not require new land conversion. Adding reasonable estimates of these remaining resources, and Easy-Hards can likely grow modestly into Net Zero.

*OK, So It’s Going to Be the Hard Way*

So by regulation and economic limits, we’re now left with Hard Feedstocks being transformed by Hard Technology into Products. Given the scale of the challenge, this is also where we’re seeing most of the Cambrian Explosion of innovation, exploration, research and project development. What is a Hard Feedstock? It is something that is simply quite far (physically or chemically) from a Product. Because of this, it usually requires Hard Technology (energy intensive, complex, innovative, sometimes unproven) to refine. If your neighbour looks at it and exclaims “That can be fuel, for real?”, it’s generally a Hard Feedstock. They also generally and hopefully tend to have large volumes available because the entire point in digging around this new world is to eventually make sure the volumes can scale. The move to Hard-Hards is very much a scale volume quest that escapes the supply constraints of the Easy Feedstock world. The major categories of Hard Feedstocks include Agricultural Waste Biomass (think wheat straw, sugarcane bagasse, rice husks), Municipal Solid Waste (MSW) and Wood (trimmings, sawdust). In Feedstocks required for e-fuels I’d include renewable electricity (for its complexity and cost) as well as CO2 (for its cost of collection and availability).

The landscape in liquid fuel Hard Technology is dizzying at the moment, and filled with more than a few 5-stalk-eyed proboscis-ed *Opabinias*, which is a compliment to innovation and not an evolutionary forecast. A quick glance at the chart shows the complex nature of recreating the fossil system. There are also some gems in the Hard Technology which stand out. Fischer-Tropsch is an established technology that works in fossil applications. Gasification is the same; gasifying coal and then synthesising it into methanol is an established, scale industry in China and the operational maturity in this fossil application should boost and accelerate its use in gasifying agricultural wastes. In fact some of the world’s largest near-operational and advanced development projects are Chinese gasification plants transforming biomass into biomethanol. 

There are some established pathways from Feedstock to Product in the Hard-Hards and project development as well as limited examples of operational capacity do tend to herd here.

* E-fuels like e-methanol and e-SAF require renewable electricity to run electrolysers which split water into oxygen and a green hydrogen, which is then synthesised with captured CO2 into e-methanol as an Intermediate; or using the Fischer-Tropsch (FT) process to build out an e-diesel, e-SAF and e-naphtha platform. E-ammonia eliminates the need for e-methanol’s CO2, simplifying the process somewhat. E-fuels may be the most daunting technology from a technical complexity and cost perspective but their success could unlock large volumes of Hard Feedstocks; solar and wind are booming and should continue to do so for decades. And while CO2 capture is young, e-fuels provide commercial use that could encourage a collection system. 
* Gasification vaporises most varieties of Hard Feedstocks into a synthetic gas (syngas) which is then either synthesised further into methanol or liquefied by the established Fischer-Tropsch process into a slate including diesel, jet fuel and naphtha. Additional green hydrogen can be added to this syngas stream (via water electrolysis) and the resulting methanol or FT slate would have some partial classification as e-fuels.  
* Cellulosic ethanol breaks down ligno-cellulosic feedstocks by hydrolysis and then ferments into a traditional ethanol Intermediate, but one which has reused a waste cellulose rather than a competing food starch.
* Biogas deserves some separate attention. Wet waste, a Hard Feedstock (for further refinement into liquid fuels) can be anaerobically digested through an Easy Technology and have its offgases collected into a biogas stream. This can be further refined into biomethane and injected to pipeline for then regular use in the existing fossil gas industry. This is established at a mature level in dairy farms via manure and in landfills and is particularly attractive due to its often industry-leading performance on carbon reduction. So stick with me here, we now have a Hard-Easy Intermediate or Product. It is then available as an Easy Feedstock for further refining via Hard Technology into eventual liquid Product; this includes creating a syngas via electric or even photocatalytic reforming, sometimes augmenting its hydrogen with additional electrolysed green hydrogen, and then liquefying into methanol or via the FT slate for a blended e- and biofuel Product. This Hard-Easy Intermediate into an Easy-Hard liquid fuel Product is a unique pathway, and remains attractive due to exceptional biogas carbon performance. As we might expect, we see a lot of innovation and funding focus on this Easy-Hard pathway and it could be an important industry in moving some Hard-Hard target volumes into this category.    

Moving beyond ethanol, biodiesel and HEFA does immediately launch us into challenging technical terrain. The energy industry has run with the Easy-Easy low carbon liquid fuels and is innovating its way into cracking the code for the Hard-Hard fuels, which are necessary to build out anything resembling a Net Zero future. It’s also the case that through time, Technologies can move in grades from Hard to Easy; in fact besides scaling volumes, the entire exercise in the liquid fuels Transition is to do just this. HEFA was at one point Hard in all three qualifications (novel, operationally immature and high capex) and has significantly advanced towards Easy as it builds capacity. 

You may have noticed I’ve remained conspicuously silent on the economics of this technology landscape, which for a commercial person, isn’t easy.
