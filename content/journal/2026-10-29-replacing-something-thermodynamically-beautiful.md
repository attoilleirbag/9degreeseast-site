It’s worth a moment of reflection on the underlying weirdness of the global human energy system. Life emerged and evolved to convert solar energy and gaseous carbon into solid carbon structures through tiny single celled plankton’s discovered route to photosynthesis and later further developed by land plants. Plankton and plants very much make air solid. Then over deep time, the debris from this life became compacted under ground and sea at scorching heats and immense pressures, effectively refining the debris into ever denser mixes of carbon based fuels; plankton mainly into liquid oil and plants into solid coal. A smart ape evolved and harnessed these dense energy reserves so thoroughly, at such scale, that their chemical combustion emitted quantities of waste Carbon Dioxide large enough to alter the planet’s atmospheric gaseous concentrations noticeably. It’s simply bad chemical luck that CO₂ vibrates at a frequency that traps Earth’s space-bound heat.

The energy available from such small volumes of fossil fuels is an almost magical characteristic of life on Earth, a subroutine of evolution; a result of photosynthesis and deep time whose hundred million year manufacturing process output could then be accessed by an intelligent animal to fuel civilisation. Try bringing that supply chain to FID.  

All of this is not to glorify fossil fuels but only to put their replacement into full context. When reviewing the entirety of the global decarbonisation effort, there are a few clear parallel directions of travel. First, electrify everything you can. Second, decarbonise any remaining dense liquid and gas fuel requirements however you can. The first pulse of activity bears downward pressure on total required liquid fuels demand; the second pushes new lower carbon liquids and gases into open competition with the remaining fossil fuel supply. Add in some carbon capture for messier distributed emissions, and presto, Net Zero. 

The second parallel of the Transition is the hard part; replacing these geologic energy dense pre-manufactured gifts of evolution and deep eras of time on Earth. When you scan the entirety of the low carbon liquid fuel market, and consider what it takes to build these molecules either from new biomass or from scratch; fermentation, esterification, gasification, electrolysis of water, CO₂ capture, methanol synthesis, Fischer-Tropsch, catalytic processing; you realise exactly how much free manufacturing fossil fuels have delivered and how much excess energy and refined tech must be found to power the replacement processing. Other than the final refining of crude oil into useful products, we are rebuilding an entirely new liquid fuel chain from current feedstocks, not reserves, that aren’t properly chemically constructed, aren’t dense and aren’t cheap. The economics follow. Oil and gas make volatile but high margins while replacement liquid fuels have to earn returns ancient biomass has been accruing for aeons. 

Even with electrification driving down total fossil fuel demand, we will still need a huge volume of these replacement fuels at Net Zero. In scale, humans currently consume 4,800 million tonnes of liquid fuels annually. This is the current oil and refined products industry. Let’s provide some scale. The actively traded portion of the crude oil market is often carried by Very Large Crude Carriers (VLCCs). Take a lovely walk along some sunny port for 4 minutes and you’ve covered a VLCC length; look up 8 stories and you’re staring at the top of the cargo space. That’s 270,000 tonnes of oil, and there are 800 of those operating at any given time. With this fleet of 800 vessels, you’ve now arrived at 2 weeks of annual human demand of oil and products fuels. 

Estimates vary but using a typical Net Zero equilibrium mapped out by the IEA, by Net Zero (estimated in 2050), humans are annually producing an oil equivalent of 350 million tonnes of biofuels, 500 million tonnes of hydrogen based efuels, and a remaining 1,200 million tonnes of residual fossil use, with 700 million tonnes for the petrochemical industry and 500 million tonnes in residual transport combustion. It is no small caveat to mention that in this scenario, carbon capture and removal is itself allowing for this large residual fossil fuel combustion and should the economics and feasibility of that effort fall short, the 850m tonnes of liquid bio and efuels would need to theoretically step up to 1,350 million tonnes, which the IEA’s own constraints suggest is impractical. There is a straightforward (if gargantuan) path to electrification displacing 2,750 million tonnes of this current 4,800 million tonnes of demand, mainly by converting road transport to Electric Vehicles. 

Electrification is the largest free ride in the Transition, an unusually downhill joyride of efficiencies and cost savings. Using energy from electricity in a motor eliminates a huge portion of waste heat, which in the combustion of fossil fuels, consumes more than 60% of the underlying energy. Make the electricity zero carbon, and you win twice, a large gain in efficiency and a parallel reduction in carbon. Even ignoring the carbon, humankind would likely have naturally moved to a more fully electrified system due to economics alone.  In this way we are intervening in the fossil fuel manufacturing facility at the photosynthesis stage, using zero-ish carbon solar, batteries, wind and nuclear electricity directly, rather than through our helpful plant and plankton intermediaries. 

However, as much as we push our electrification agenda, which benefits from large efficiency tailwinds, there will remain places it cannot work and this is where our residual 850 million tonnes of required Net Zero low carbon liquid fuels must be manufactured. Mainly this is the isolated air and sea environments where charging is unavailable and battery density is unlikely to reach useful efficiencies, as well as some extremely energy hungry industrial processes. There are no charging points in the mid-Pacific. The boundary of these worlds, between electrifiable energy and this bedrock of permanent high density requirements will likely blur through time, and economics of both solutions will battle it out to establish something resembling a stable border. 

So here we are. The Liquid Fuel decarbonisation effort is looking to build a fully realised supply chain of low or zero carbon fuels sized at an oil-equivalent 850 million tonnes by Net Zero. We have built out more than 100 million tonnes of oil-equivalent biofuels capacity in the last 20 years, leaving 750 million tonnes to go. If we judge an ambitiously typical world-scale low carbon liquid plant at 500,000 tonnes of product, and adjust for lower energy density of the replacement fuels, there’s a remaining scope of 2,000 of these low carbon liquid fuel plants to build; 87 of these plants made operational, annually for the next 23 years. Assuming Net Zero at 2075, and we’re cutting ribbons on 42 of these facilities each year. To put it delicately, this is a challenge. And we’ve plucked the low-lying carbon fruit; moving beyond the current simpler technologies and their constrained feedstocks, almost all of the 2,000 plants will require either a technically challenging gasification setup to access waste biomass or an electrolysis driven efuels setup. Against the electrification’s joyride, we’re now in a gruelling uphill climb against efficiency, technology risk, and cost. 

This should help contextualise the overwhelming effectiveness of electrification efforts and provide the scale intuition we need to understand the current state of low carbon liquid fuels infiltration, as they battle against nature’s perfect, dense and cheap fossil fuels. To be fair, the competition has had a few hundred million years head start. 

Part 2 of this essay will cover the layout of technical choices we have in delivering these 2,000 plants.
