It’s worth a moment of reflection on theunderlying weirdness of the global human energy system. Life emerged andevolved to convert solar energy and gaseous carbon into solid carbon structuresthrough tiny single celled plankton’s discovered route to photosynthesis andlater further developed by land plants. Plankton and plants very much make airsolid. Then over deep time, the debris from this life became compacted under groundand sea at scorching heats and immense pressures, effectively refining thedebris into ever denser mixes of carbon based fuels; plankton mainly intoliquid oil and plants into solid coal. A smart ape evolved and harnessed thesedense energy reserves so thoroughly, at such scale, that their chemicalcombustion emitted quantities of waste Carbon Dioxide large enough to alter theplanet’s atmospheric gaseous concentrations noticeably. It’s simply bad chemicalluck that CO₂ vibrates at a frequency that traps Earth’s space-bound heat.

The energy available from such smallvolumes of fossil fuels is an almost magical characteristic of life on Earth, asubroutine of evolution; a result of photosynthesis and deep time whose hundredmillion year manufacturing process output could then be accessed by anintelligent animal to fuel civilisation. Try bringing that supply chain toFID.  

All of this is not to glorify fossil fuelsbut only to put their replacement into full context. When reviewing theentirety of the global decarbonisation effort, there are a few clear parallel directionsof travel. First, electrify everything you can. Second, decarbonise anyremaining dense liquid and gas fuel requirements however you can. The firstpulse of activity bears downward pressure on total required liquid fuelsdemand; the second pushes new lower carbon liquids and gases into opencompetition with the remaining fossil fuel supply. Add in some carbon capturefor messier distributed emissions, and presto, Net Zero. 

The second parallel of the Transition isthe hard part; replacing these geologic energy dense pre-manufactured gifts of evolutionand deep eras of time on Earth. When you scan the entirety of the low carbonliquid fuel market, and consider what it takes to build these molecules eitherfrom new biomass or from scratch; fermentation, esterification, gasification, electrolysisof water, CO₂ capture, methanol synthesis, Fischer-Tropsch, catalyticprocessing; you realise exactly how much free manufacturing fossil fuels havedelivered and how much excess energy and refined tech must be found to powerthe replacement processing. Other than the final refining of crude oil intouseful products, we are rebuilding an entirely new liquid fuel chain fromcurrent feedstocks, not reserves, that aren’t properly chemically constructed,aren’t dense and aren’t cheap. The economics follow. Oil and gas make volatilebut high margins while replacement liquid fuels have to earn returns ancientbiomass has been accruing for aeons. 

Even with electrification driving downtotal fossil fuel demand, we will still need a huge volume of these replacementfuels at Net Zero. In scale, humans currently consume 4,800 million tonnes ofliquid fuels annually. This is the current oil and refined products industry. Let’sprovide some scale. The actively traded portion of the crude oil market is oftencarried by Very Large Crude Carriers (VLCCs). Take a lovely walk along some sunnyport for 4 minutes and you’ve covered a VLCC length; look up 8 stories andyou’re staring at the top of the cargo space. That’s 270,000 tonnes of oil, andthere are 800 of those operating at any given time. With this fleet of 800vessels, you’ve now arrived at 2 weeks of annual human demand of oil andproducts fuels. 

Estimates vary but using a typical Net Zeroequilibrium mapped out by the IEA, by Net Zero (estimated in 2050), humans are annuallyproducing an oil equivalent of 350 million tonnes of biofuels, 500 million tonnesof hydrogen based efuels, and a remaining 1,200 million tonnes of residualfossil use, with 700 million tonnes for the petrochemical industry and 500 milliontonnes in residual transport combustion. It is no small caveat to mention thatin this scenario, carbon capture and removal is itself allowing for this largeresidual fossil fuel combustion and should the economics and feasibility ofthat effort fall short, the 850m tonnes of liquid bio and efuels would need totheoretically step up to 1,350 million tonnes, which the IEA’s own constraintssuggest is impractical. There is a straightforward (if gargantuan) path toelectrification displacing 2,750 million tonnes of this current 4,800 milliontonnes of demand, mainly by converting road transport to Electric Vehicles. 

Electrification is the largest free ride inthe Transition, an unusually downhill joyride of efficiencies and cost savings.Using energy from electricity in a motor eliminates a huge portion of wasteheat, which in the combustion of fossil fuels, consumes more than 60% of theunderlying energy. Make the electricity zero carbon, and you win twice, a largegain in efficiency and a parallel reduction in carbon. Even ignoring thecarbon, humankind would likely have naturally moved to a more fully electrifiedsystem due to economics alone.  In thisway we are intervening in the fossil fuel manufacturing facility at thephotosynthesis stage, using zero-ish carbon solar, batteries, wind and nuclearelectricity directly, rather than through our helpful plant and plankton intermediaries.

However, as much as we push ourelectrification agenda, which benefits from large efficiency tailwinds, therewill remain places it cannot work and this is where our residual 850 milliontonnes of required Net Zero low carbon liquid fuels must be manufactured.Mainly this is the isolated air and sea environments where charging isunavailable and battery density is unlikely to reach useful efficiencies, aswell as some extremely energy hungry industrial processes. There are nocharging points in the mid-Pacific. The boundary of these worlds, betweenelectrifiable energy and this bedrock of permanent high density requirementswill likely blur through time, and economics of both solutions will battle itout to establish something resembling a stable border. 

So here we are. The Liquid Fueldecarbonisation effort is looking to build a fully realised supply chain of lowor zero carbon fuels sized at an oil-equivalent 850 million tonnes by Net Zero.We have built out more than 100 million tonnes of oil-equivalent biofuelscapacity in the last 20 years, leaving 750 million tonnes to go. If we judge anambitiously typical world-scale low carbon liquid plant at 500,000 tonnes ofproduct, and adjust for lower energy density of the replacement fuels, there’s aremaining scope of 2,000 of these low carbon liquid fuel plants to build; 87 ofthese plants made operational, annually for the next 23 years. Assuming NetZero at 2075, and we’re cutting ribbons on 42 of these facilities each year. Toput it delicately, this is a challenge. And we’ve plucked the low-lying carbon fruit;moving beyond the current simpler technologies and their constrainedfeedstocks, almost all of the 2,000 plants will require either a technicallychallenging gasification setup to access waste biomass or an electrolysisdriven efuels setup. Against the electrification’s joyride, we’re now in agruelling uphill climb against efficiency, technology risk, and cost. 

This should help contextualise theoverwhelming effectiveness of electrification efforts and provide the scale intuitionwe need to understand the current state of low carbon liquid fuelsinfiltration, as they battle against nature’s perfect, dense and cheap fossilfuels. To be fair, the competition has had a few hundred million years headstart.
